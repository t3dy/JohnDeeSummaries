Essay

This chapter completes Harkness's sequence by moving from reading nature to healing nature. Its details matter because they show that Dee's alchemical concerns were medical, restorative, theological, and eschatological as much as metallurgical. The chapter's immediate argument is: Completes the program: after reading nature through cabala, Dee receives restored Adamic alchemy and the medicine of God to heal nature's corruption.

Harkness argues that John Dee's angel conversations were not an embarrassing late-career aberration, a collapse into credulity, or a body of material external to his natural philosophy. They were a sustained continuation of his project as a Christian natural philosopher. The angelic sessions addressed the same problems that animated Dee's mathematical, astronomical, cabalistic, alchemical, and bibliographical work: how to read the Book of Nature, how to restore corrupted knowledge, how to reconcile human learning with divine revelation, and how to act when nature and time seemed to be approaching their apocalyptic end. That thesis matters because it prevents the reader from treating the angel diaries as an eccentric appendix to Dee's real career.

Her central conceptual move is to treat the natural world as a text in Dee's thought. For Dee, the Book of Nature had been damaged by the Fall, by historical decay, and by the approaching end of the world. Ordinary scholarly methods could no longer read it securely. Angelic revelation supplied what human learning lacked: a divine language, a true cabala of nature, and a medicine of God capable of interpreting and restoring nature. The chapter therefore reads Dee's conversations as practices embedded in books, instruments, households, patrons, and late Renaissance categories of knowledge.

Places alchemy and Paracelsian medicine within natural philosophy; argues Dee's alchemy is restorative and eschatological, not merely gold-making. Harkness is arguing about what historians are allowed to count as natural philosophy in a world where Scripture, nature, angels, mathematics, cabala, alchemy, medicine, and apocalyptic expectation could belong to a single intellectual project.

Chapter 6 completes the sequence from diagnosis to interpretation to cure. Once Dee could read the Book of Nature through divine language and true cabala, he still needed a way to heal nature's corruption. The angels supplied Adam's alchemy, the medicine of God.

Harkness begins by placing alchemy within early modern natural philosophy. Alchemy promised transformation, perfection, healing, and restoration. It was disreputable in some contexts, but it was also a serious field for natural philosophers, physicians, Paracelsians, court practitioners, and reformers. Harkness draws on scholarship on alchemy and chemical medicine to show that Dee's hopes were not eccentric in kind, even if his angelic source was unusual.

The chapter argues that alchemy responded to the same problem as cabala: the sickness of nature. The world was decaying; human bodies were corruptible; knowledge had deteriorated; ordinary alchemical practice had also decayed. Dee needed not merely alchemy but restored alchemy, an art as Adam had known it.

Paracelsus is central here. Harkness connects Dee's medicine of God to Paracelsian themes of true medicine, natural signatures, divine healing, and reform of medical-alchemical knowledge. Dee owned and annotated Paracelsian works. The angelic medicine therefore belongs to a larger sixteenth-century conversation about chemical medicine, not just to a private visionary system.

Harkness also connects alchemy back to cabala. The true foundation for searching nature is the cabala revealed by God. Cabala reads the hidden text; alchemy acts on it. Together they form the interpretive and restorative sciences of Dee's angelic natural philosophy.

The chapter then analyzes specific angelic revelations and visions about alchemy. Angels speak of powers in earth, dissolution, death, rebirth, corruption, and the secrets of nature. Harkness treats these not as random symbolic pageantry but as alchemical pedagogy. The images of birds, death, transformation, planetary frames, and resurrection connect alchemical process with apocalyptic transformation. Dee and Kelley themselves are sometimes drawn into the symbolism as agents or subjects of transformation.

Adam's alchemy also has cosmic and eschatological implications. It is not merely about producing gold. Its highest goal is the restitution of nature and the transformation of corruptible bodies. Harkness ties this to Pauline resurrection language and to the apocalyptic promise that bodies and nature will be changed at the end.

The named people, books, places, instruments, and religious tensions are not background color; they are part of the mechanism by which Dee's project worked. When the chapter discusses terms such as alchemy; medicine of God; Paracelsus; restitution; corruption; resurrection; Adam, those terms should be read as coordinates in a larger map of Dee's knowledge-making.

Harkness treats nature as a damaged text that Dee believed had to be read, corrected, and in some sense restored. That premise lets her connect apparently separate materials: mathematical optics, hieroglyphic signs, angelic voices, true names, tables of letters, prophetic dates, alchemical medicines, and the politics of Christian reform.

Harkness is not claiming that Dee was secretly a modern scientist, nor that every angelic utterance was internally consistent, nor that the conversations succeeded on their own terms. She is claiming that the conversations were historically coherent inside sixteenth-century Christian natural philosophy, where divine revelation could appear as a remedy for the limits of fallen human learning.

Harkness is responding to several inherited interpretive habits: Against that inherited split, Harkness makes the angel conversations central evidence for Dee's intellectual life rather than evidence of intellectual collapse.

- The rationalist split between Dee's "serious" scientific work and his supposedly irrational angel diaries. John Heilbron and Wayne Shumaker are examples of scholarship that treats Dee's angelic work as gullibility or a lapse from natural-philosophical lucidity. - The partial rehabilitation of Dee by Frances Yates and later Renaissance occultism scholarship, which recognized intellectual richness in Hermetic, cabalistic, and magical traditions but did not always integrate the angel diaries fully into Dee's natural philosophy. - Nicholas Clulee's important reconstruction of Dee's natural philosophy, which Harkness builds on but revises. Clulee connects Dee to earlier natural-philosophical traditions, but Harkness argues that he still leaves the angel conversations too far outside the category of natural philosophy. - Christopher Whitby's manuscript work on the early spirit actions, which Harkness treats as foundational for chronology, participants, and scrying practice, while extending the analysis into wider intellectual and cultural history. - William Sherman's work on Dee as reader, writer, annotator, and political communicator, which supports Harkness's emphasis on marginalia, books, readership, and the social circulation of Dee's ideas. Harkness borrows the seriousness with which that scholarship treats esoteric traditions, but she keeps the analysis close to Dee's manuscripts, marginalia, material objects, and conversational records.

The chapter asks readers to suspend later disciplinary boundaries long enough to see how knowledge was organized before the modern category of science hardened. In that setting, prayer could discipline inquiry, angels could mediate knowledge, cabala could interpret nature, alchemy could promise medicine, and apocalypse could define urgency.

This chapter supplies a lens for moving between the summary cards, the daybook entries, the spirit actions, the primary-source subsections, and Casaubon's later printed framing. It asks the reader to track continuity: Dee's questions about language, authority, mediation, natural decay, patronage, and reform recur across the entire collection.

The chapter should not be reduced to its most spectacular angelic details. Its argument depends on slow accumulation: how Dee defines legitimate authority, how he distinguishes divine assistance from illicit coercion, how he turns textual corruption into a problem about nature, and how he makes social risk part of intellectual practice. Those details are precisely what make Harkness's reading historiographical rather than merely descriptive.
