Essay

This chapter reconstructs the intellectual prehistory of the angel conversations and therefore carries much of the burden of Harkness's continuity argument. Its historical details matter because they show that the later spirit diaries did not replace Dee's mathematics, optics, cabala, hieroglyphics, and textual scholarship; they extended pressures already present in those fields. The chapter's immediate argument is: Explains how Dee's earlier work prepared the angel conversations: Scripture and Nature as books, Propaedeumata as optical/celestial causation, Monas as symbolic decipherment, and mathematics as mediator.

Harkness argues that John Dee's angel conversations were not an embarrassing late-career aberration, a collapse into credulity, or a body of material external to his natural philosophy. They were a sustained continuation of his project as a Christian natural philosopher. The angelic sessions addressed the same problems that animated Dee's mathematical, astronomical, cabalistic, alchemical, and bibliographical work: how to read the Book of Nature, how to restore corrupted knowledge, how to reconcile human learning with divine revelation, and how to act when nature and time seemed to be approaching their apocalyptic end. That thesis matters because it prevents the reader from treating the angel diaries as an eccentric appendix to Dee's real career.

Her central conceptual move is to treat the natural world as a text in Dee's thought. For Dee, the Book of Nature had been damaged by the Fall, by historical decay, and by the approaching end of the world. Ordinary scholarly methods could no longer read it securely. Angelic revelation supplied what human learning lacked: a divine language, a true cabala of nature, and a medicine of God capable of interpreting and restoring nature. The chapter therefore reads Dee's conversations as practices embedded in books, instruments, households, patrons, and late Renaissance categories of knowledge.

Argues continuity between Dee's accepted natural-philosophical works and angelic work; links humanist textual crisis with natural-philosophical uncertainty. Harkness is arguing about what historians are allowed to count as natural philosophy in a world where Scripture, nature, angels, mathematics, cabala, alchemy, medicine, and apocalyptic expectation could belong to a single intellectual project.

Chapter 2 turns backward from Prague to explain how Dee constructed the intellectual ladder that made angelic conversation plausible. The chapter's basic problem is this: if Dee wanted certain knowledge of nature and salvation, what resources did he have before the angels?

Harkness argues that Dee's crisis was linked to a broader Renaissance humanist crisis of authority. Humanists had exposed textual corruption, historical error, mistransmission, and the unreliability of inherited learning. This crisis applied not only to Scripture and ancient texts but also, in Dee's thought, to nature itself. If the Book of Nature was also damaged, then natural philosophy required new methods.

The chapter develops three major stages in Dee's pre-angelic natural philosophy.

First, Dee laid foundations in the Books of Scripture and Nature. Harkness explains the two-book tradition: God revealed himself in Scripture and in creation. Dee accepted both, but the Book of Nature presented special problems. What language was it written in? How could fallen humans read it? How had decay, celestial change, and historical degeneration affected it? Harkness links these questions to biblical passages about cosmic aging, to humanist textual anxiety, and to Renaissance belief in the deterioration of knowledge.

Second, Dee attempted to observe the Book of Nature through the *Propaedeumata Aphoristica*. Harkness reads this work not as isolated technical astronomy but as an effort to understand celestial-terrestrial causation through optics, rays, species, and light. Dee drew on traditions associated with al-Kindi, Grosseteste, Bacon, Ficino, and others. Radiation linked the heavens and earth, making celestial influence physically and metaphysically intelligible. This mattered because it gave Dee a way to study invisible or occult causation without abandoning natural philosophy.

Third, Dee attempted to decipher the Book of Nature through the *Monas Hieroglyphica*. The *Monas* is treated as a symbolic and cabalistic tool for reading nature. Harkness connects Dee's monad to hieroglyphic theory, Christian cabala, alchemy, mathematics, and Pantheus's *Voarchadumia*. The monad sought to compress cosmic order into a single sign. It did not solve all of Dee's problems, but it shows his movement from observation toward symbolic decipherment.

Harkness then brings in Dee's "Mathematical Preface" to Euclid. Mathematics occupies an intermediate cosmic position: it deals with intelligible, stable, incorruptible forms while still having applications in material things. This made mathematics a bridge between the corrupt natural world and the supernatural. Dee's praise of mathematics is therefore not merely pedagogical or practical; it is metaphysical.

The chapter's conclusion is that Dee's angel conversations did not come out of nowhere. They extended preexisting concerns: the relation between Scripture and nature, the unreliability of fallen knowledge, the promise of ancient wisdom, the power of mathematics and signs, and the need for a method to cross from the visible to the invisible.

The named people, books, places, instruments, and religious tensions are not background color; they are part of the mechanism by which Dee's project worked. When the chapter discusses terms such as Jacob's ladder; Scripture; Propaedeumata; Monas; mathematics; optics; cabala, those terms should be read as coordinates in a larger map of Dee's knowledge-making.

Harkness treats nature as a damaged text that Dee believed had to be read, corrected, and in some sense restored. That premise lets her connect apparently separate materials: mathematical optics, hieroglyphic signs, angelic voices, true names, tables of letters, prophetic dates, alchemical medicines, and the politics of Christian reform.

Harkness is not claiming that Dee was secretly a modern scientist, nor that every angelic utterance was internally consistent, nor that the conversations succeeded on their own terms. She is claiming that the conversations were historically coherent inside sixteenth-century Christian natural philosophy, where divine revelation could appear as a remedy for the limits of fallen human learning.

Harkness is responding to several inherited interpretive habits: Against that inherited split, Harkness makes the angel conversations central evidence for Dee's intellectual life rather than evidence of intellectual collapse.

- The rationalist split between Dee's "serious" scientific work and his supposedly irrational angel diaries. John Heilbron and Wayne Shumaker are examples of scholarship that treats Dee's angelic work as gullibility or a lapse from natural-philosophical lucidity. - The partial rehabilitation of Dee by Frances Yates and later Renaissance occultism scholarship, which recognized intellectual richness in Hermetic, cabalistic, and magical traditions but did not always integrate the angel diaries fully into Dee's natural philosophy. - Nicholas Clulee's important reconstruction of Dee's natural philosophy, which Harkness builds on but revises. Clulee connects Dee to earlier natural-philosophical traditions, but Harkness argues that he still leaves the angel conversations too far outside the category of natural philosophy. - Christopher Whitby's manuscript work on the early spirit actions, which Harkness treats as foundational for chronology, participants, and scrying practice, while extending the analysis into wider intellectual and cultural history. - William Sherman's work on Dee as reader, writer, annotator, and political communicator, which supports Harkness's emphasis on marginalia, books, readership, and the social circulation of Dee's ideas. Harkness borrows the seriousness with which that scholarship treats esoteric traditions, but she keeps the analysis close to Dee's manuscripts, marginalia, material objects, and conversational records.

The chapter asks readers to suspend later disciplinary boundaries long enough to see how knowledge was organized before the modern category of science hardened. In that setting, prayer could discipline inquiry, angels could mediate knowledge, cabala could interpret nature, alchemy could promise medicine, and apocalypse could define urgency.

It asks the reader to track continuity: Dee's questions about language, authority, mediation, natural decay, patronage, and reform recur across the entire collection.
