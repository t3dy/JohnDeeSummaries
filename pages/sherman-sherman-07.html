The chapter is therefore not just biographical narrative. It asks what sort of evidence historians should privilege when Dee appears as reader, writer, political client, occult philosopher, imperial projector, courtier, or suspected conjuror.

The chapter gathers Dee's major imperial writings: General and Rare Memorials, Of Famous and Rich Discoveries, Brytanici Imperii Limites, and THALATTOKRATIA BRETTANIKI. Sherman reads them as connected pieces of an evolving maritime-political argument.

Dee's rhetoric of discovery is also rhetoric of recovery. Historical and legendary materials are used to claim that English maritime expansion restores older British rights rather than inventing new ambitions.

The chapter shows Dee's mixture of scholarship and advocacy. Philology, antiquarian history, cartography, navigation, law, and counsel are marshaled for Elizabethan oceanic policy.

These arguments are strongest when they explain how those traces were produced and why they mattered.

Finally, the chapter belongs to a larger scholarly correction. Dee was not only a thinker with doctrines; he was a practitioner moving through institutions, households, libraries, courts, churches, and European learned networks. That social motion is part of the intellectual history.
