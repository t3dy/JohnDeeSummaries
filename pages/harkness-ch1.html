Essay

This chapter deliberately begins in Prague in 1586 because Harkness wants Dee's angelic project to appear first as a public, European, political, and confessional event. The chapter's most important historical detail is the relocation of Dee from the stereotype of the solitary Mortlake magus into a network of imperial, Lutheran, Catholic, alchemical, and prophetic audiences. The chapter's immediate argument is: Uses Prague to show Dee as public prophet, communicator, natural philosopher, and political-religious actor. Reconstructs Dee, Kelley, scryers, household, instruments, revealed books, angels, patrons, and audiences.

Harkness argues that John Dee's angel conversations were not an embarrassing late-career aberration, a collapse into credulity, or a body of material external to his natural philosophy. They were a sustained continuation of his project as a Christian natural philosopher. The angelic sessions addressed the same problems that animated Dee's mathematical, astronomical, cabalistic, alchemical, and bibliographical work: how to read the Book of Nature, how to restore corrupted knowledge, how to reconcile human learning with divine revelation, and how to act when nature and time seemed to be approaching their apocalyptic end. That thesis matters because it prevents the reader from treating the angel diaries as an eccentric appendix to Dee's real career.

Her central conceptual move is to treat the natural world as a text in Dee's thought. For Dee, the Book of Nature had been damaged by the Fall, by historical decay, and by the approaching end of the world. Ordinary scholarly methods could no longer read it securely. Angelic revelation supplied what human learning lacked: a divine language, a true cabala of nature, and a medicine of God capable of interpreting and restoring nature. The chapter therefore reads Dee's conversations as practices embedded in books, instruments, households, patrons, and late Renaissance categories of knowledge.

Replaces private-eccentric Dee with socially embedded Dee; treats scrying, material culture, household practice, and patronage as part of knowledge production. Harkness is arguing about what historians are allowed to count as natural philosophy in a world where Scripture, nature, angels, mathematics, cabala, alchemy, medicine, and apocalyptic expectation could belong to a single intellectual project.

Chapter 1 begins in Prague in 1586 rather than with Dee's youth. Harkness uses the Bohemian Lutheran Budovec's account of Dee to unsettle the familiar image of Dee as merely the isolated "magus of Mortlake." In Prague, Dee appears as prophet, ambassador, religious provocateur, and public communicator. This opening makes the angel conversations social and political from the outset.

The chapter's first major claim is that Dee's conversations were not private eccentricities. They had audiences. In Prague, Dee's claims about miraculous reformation, Rome, Constantinople, and Christian renewal circulated among religious and political observers. The conversations therefore belonged to a European public sphere of prophecy, patronage, religious anxiety, and imperial politics.

Harkness then reconstructs the participants.

Dee is presented as a learned natural philosopher, courtly adviser, reader, collector, mathematician, astrologer, and communicator. His early education at Chelmsford, Cambridge, Louvain, Paris, and Continental centers of learning placed him in contact with major mathematical and humanist traditions. Harkness emphasizes his mobility and networks: Mercator, Gemma Frisius, Commandino, Aldrovandi, and others matter because Dee's natural philosophy was never simply local.

Edward Kelley is treated with care rather than reduced to fraud. Harkness acknowledges suspicion, volatility, possible deceit, alchemical ambition, and repeated conflict, but she also insists that Kelley was structurally indispensable. He supplied visions Dee could not see. Their partnership was unstable, but the instability itself shaped the conversations. Kelley's later alchemical reputation and patronage career complicate the idea that he was only Dee's tool.

Barnabas Saul, Bartholomew Hickman, Arthur Dee, and possibly Jane Dee are part of a broader scrying ecology. Harkness uses them to show that scrying was collaborative, mediated, and household-based. Dee's dependence on scryers makes the conversations less like solitary revelation and more like an experimental and devotional practice distributed across a group.

Harkness gives special attention to revealed books: *Liber Logaeth*, the *Claves angelicae*, *Liber scientiae auxilii et victoriae terrestris*, and Dee's fascination with the *Book of Soyga*. The point is that the angel conversations produced texts as well as visions. Dee's angelic project was bibliographical, linguistic, and material.

The chapter's account of angels is also important. Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, and Uriel are not generic supernatural beings. Harkness situates them in Christian, Jewish, and cabalistic angelological traditions. Uriel's connection to hidden wisdom and Enochic traditions matters especially for Dee's later concern with divine language and lost books.

The final part of the chapter turns to audience and danger. Dee's circle included patrons and observers such as Laski, Rudolf II, Pucci, Catholic officials, Jesuits, and papal nuncios. Dee's revelations had to be authenticated in a divided Reformation world. Protestants and Catholics disagreed over private revelation, miracles, prophecy, and spiritual authority. The same claims that made Dee compelling also made him dangerous.

The named people, books, places, instruments, and religious tensions are not background color; they are part of the mechanism by which Dee's project worked. When the chapter discusses terms such as Prague; Budovec; Kelley; scryers; showstone; holy table; Liber Logaeth; Rudolf II; Pucci, those terms should be read as coordinates in a larger map of Dee's knowledge-making.

Harkness treats nature as a damaged text that Dee believed had to be read, corrected, and in some sense restored. That premise lets her connect apparently separate materials: mathematical optics, hieroglyphic signs, angelic voices, true names, tables of letters, prophetic dates, alchemical medicines, and the politics of Christian reform.

Harkness is not claiming that Dee was secretly a modern scientist, nor that every angelic utterance was internally consistent, nor that the conversations succeeded on their own terms. She is claiming that the conversations were historically coherent inside sixteenth-century Christian natural philosophy, where divine revelation could appear as a remedy for the limits of fallen human learning.

Harkness is responding to several inherited interpretive habits: Against that inherited split, Harkness makes the angel conversations central evidence for Dee's intellectual life rather than evidence of intellectual collapse.

- The rationalist split between Dee's "serious" scientific work and his supposedly irrational angel diaries. John Heilbron and Wayne Shumaker are examples of scholarship that treats Dee's angelic work as gullibility or a lapse from natural-philosophical lucidity. - The partial rehabilitation of Dee by Frances Yates and later Renaissance occultism scholarship, which recognized intellectual richness in Hermetic, cabalistic, and magical traditions but did not always integrate the angel diaries fully into Dee's natural philosophy. - Nicholas Clulee's important reconstruction of Dee's natural philosophy, which Harkness builds on but revises. Clulee connects Dee to earlier natural-philosophical traditions, but Harkness argues that he still leaves the angel conversations too far outside the category of natural philosophy. - Christopher Whitby's manuscript work on the early spirit actions, which Harkness treats as foundational for chronology, participants, and scrying practice, while extending the analysis into wider intellectual and cultural history. - William Sherman's work on Dee as reader, writer, annotator, and political communicator, which supports Harkness's emphasis on marginalia, books, readership, and the social circulation of Dee's ideas. Harkness borrows the seriousness with which that scholarship treats esoteric traditions, but she keeps the analysis close to Dee's manuscripts, marginalia, material objects, and conversational records.

The chapter asks readers to suspend later disciplinary boundaries long enough to see how knowledge was organized before the modern category of science hardened. In that setting, prayer could discipline inquiry, angels could mediate knowledge, cabala could interpret nature, alchemy could promise medicine, and apocalypse could define urgency.

This chapter supplies a lens for moving between the summary cards, the daybook entries, the spirit actions, the primary-source subsections, and Casaubon's later printed framing. It asks the reader to track continuity: Dee's questions about language, authority, mediation, natural decay, patronage, and reform recur across the entire collection.

Its argument depends on slow accumulation: how Dee defines legitimate authority, how he distinguishes divine assistance from illicit coercion, how he turns textual corruption into a problem about nature, and how he makes social risk part of intellectual practice.
