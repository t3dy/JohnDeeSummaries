Essay

The epilogue asks what happens when Dee's angelic project fails socially and politically but survives materially, textually, and historiographically. Its details matter because later reception, especially Casaubon's framing, helped create the very interpretive problem Harkness is trying to undo. Harkness argues that John Dee's angel conversations were not an embarrassing late-career aberration, a collapse into credulity, or a body of material external to his natural philosophy. They were a sustained continuation of his project as a Christian natural philosopher. The angelic sessions addressed the same problems that animated Dee's mathematical, astronomical, cabalistic, alchemical, and bibliographical work: how to read the Book of Nature, how to restore corrupted knowledge, how to reconcile human learning with divine revelation, and how to act when nature and time seemed to be approaching their apocalyptic end. That thesis matters because it prevents the reader from treating the angel diaries as an eccentric appendix to Dee's real career.

Her central conceptual move is to treat the natural world as a text in Dee's thought. For Dee, the Book of Nature had been damaged by the Fall, by historical decay, and by the approaching end of the world. Ordinary scholarly methods could no longer read it securely. Angelic revelation supplied what human learning lacked: a divine language, a true cabala of nature, and a medicine of God capable of interpreting and restoring nature. The chapter therefore reads Dee's conversations as practices embedded in books, instruments, households, patrons, and late Renaissance categories of knowledge.

Explains how reception history made the angel conversations seem marginal; contrasts Casaubon's warning frame with continued interest from Ashmole, Hooke, Boyle, and others. Harkness is arguing about what historians are allowed to count as natural philosophy in a world where Scripture, nature, angels, mathematics, cabala, alchemy, medicine, and apocalyptic expectation could belong to a single intellectual project.

The epilogue traces the collapse and afterlife of Dee's angelic project. The restored religion, cabala, alchemy, and medicine of God did not heal the relationship between Dee and Kelley. After 1585 the surviving conversations are sparse. Kelley became increasingly absorbed in independent laboratory work and noble patronage, especially in Central Europe. Dee and Kelley separated in 1589.

Harkness emphasizes the sadness and ambiguity of Dee's later years. The promised cosmic restitution did not occur in the expected form. Prophetic dates failed. Dee returned to England, pursued patronage, endured poverty and declining status, and continued to hope for recognition. He remained committed to the angelic project even when its worldly results were disappointing.

The epilogue also examines manuscript survival and reception. Dee's death, the dispersal of his library, the uncertain fate of his instruments, and the later handling of the diaries shaped how historians came to know him. Casaubon's *True and Faithful Relation* was especially influential because it published the diaries as a warning against spirits and enthusiasm. This editorial frame encouraged later readers to separate Dee's angel conversations from his intellectual achievements.

Harkness then follows a different reception line. Seventeenth-century natural philosophers and antiquaries such as Ashmole, Hooke, Boyle, and others could be interested in Dee's materials even when they disagreed with Casaubon's moral framing. The angel diaries, holy table, and related objects entered antiquarian, alchemical, and natural-philosophical networks. Dee's reputation was therefore not simply one of disgrace; the materials continued to matter to later knowledge-makers.

The epilogue returns to the historiographical problem. Dee's conversations have often been diminished because they are hard to classify. They are neither modern science nor simple superstition. Harkness argues that they should be understood as Christian natural philosophy practiced under conditions of textual corruption, apocalyptic urgency, and hope for divine revelation.

Harkness treats nature as a damaged text that Dee believed had to be read, corrected, and in some sense restored. That premise lets her connect apparently separate materials: mathematical optics, hieroglyphic signs, angelic voices, true names, tables of letters, prophetic dates, alchemical medicines, and the politics of Christian reform.

Harkness is not claiming that Dee was secretly a modern scientist, nor that every angelic utterance was internally consistent, nor that the conversations succeeded on their own terms. She is claiming that the conversations were historically coherent inside sixteenth-century Christian natural philosophy, where divine revelation could appear as a remedy for the limits of fallen human learning.

Harkness is responding to several inherited interpretive habits: Against that inherited split, Harkness makes the angel conversations central evidence for Dee's intellectual life rather than evidence of intellectual collapse.

- The rationalist split between Dee's "serious" scientific work and his supposedly irrational angel diaries. John Heilbron and Wayne Shumaker are examples of scholarship that treats Dee's angelic work as gullibility or a lapse from natural-philosophical lucidity. - The partial rehabilitation of Dee by Frances Yates and later Renaissance occultism scholarship, which recognized intellectual richness in Hermetic, cabalistic, and magical traditions but did not always integrate the angel diaries fully into Dee's natural philosophy. - Nicholas Clulee's important reconstruction of Dee's natural philosophy, which Harkness builds on but revises. Clulee connects Dee to earlier natural-philosophical traditions, but Harkness argues that he still leaves the angel conversations too far outside the category of natural philosophy. - Christopher Whitby's manuscript work on the early spirit actions, which Harkness treats as foundational for chronology, participants, and scrying practice, while extending the analysis into wider intellectual and cultural history. - William Sherman's work on Dee as reader, writer, annotator, and political communicator, which supports Harkness's emphasis on marginalia, books, readership, and the social circulation of Dee's ideas. Harkness borrows the seriousness with which that scholarship treats esoteric traditions, but she keeps the analysis close to Dee's manuscripts, marginalia, material objects, and conversational records.

The chapter asks readers to suspend later disciplinary boundaries long enough to see how knowledge was organized before the modern category of science hardened. In that setting, prayer could discipline inquiry, angels could mediate knowledge, cabala could interpret nature, alchemy could promise medicine, and apocalypse could define urgency.
